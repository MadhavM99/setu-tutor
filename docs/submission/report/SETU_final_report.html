Final report · 15 November 2026 · Token Guessers (LMA course project)

SETU: a Hindi-first Telugu tutor in which the learner teaches an AI classmate

Rule: models write, rules decide. What we built, what we measured on five model capabilities, where it failed, and what it cost.

Team: Madhav Malhotra, Kushal Karan, Faviana Noronha, Shaik Areesh · Code: github.com/MadhavM99/setu-tutor (branch faviana-noronha) · mid-submission report: docs/submission/report/SETU_mid_submission_report.pdf

Summary

  • Built: a working Hindi-interface tutor (FastAPI, PostgreSQL) with a six-level path from Telugu letters to conversations, a classmate (Raju) who writes sentences with one planted Hindi habit, a hint-only coach, and a teacher console. 1,287 tests pass.
  • Control held: no model decided a grade or changed memory (0 model-decided verdicts in the multi-agent setup against 65 in the single agent); the checker was not fooled by 25 tricks. One check failed: the unsure rate was 0.18 against a ceiling of 0.15.
  • Capabilities: tool calling made Raju's sentences valid 0.93–0.97 of the time against 0.72 for prompting alone, at about 7.5 times the tokens. Stepwise reasoning, retrieval (RAG) and multi-agent roles did not improve accuracy; fine-tuning was a no-go for lack of data (23 usable pairs, 200 needed).
  • Honest limits: no learner has used the system, the two simulations disagree on whether correcting Raju helps, the checker agrees with native labels less than a prompt-only judge (κ 0.15 against 0.46), and no native speaker has reviewed the Telugu.

1. Problem statement and goals

Problem statement

Hindi speakers learning Telugu bring knowledge that helps (shared Sanskrit words, letters that line up almost one to one) and habits that hurt (the Hindi question word at the start of a yes/no question, Hindi-style negation, carried-over gender agreement). Most language apps ignore both. Two things block a tutor for this pair. First, a beginner cannot correct anyone until they know enough Telugu, so a "teach the classmate" tutor needs an on-ramp. Second, a language model asked to play a weak classmate knows too much: it leaks correct forms and words the learner has not met. We build SETU around one rule, models write, rules decide. The classmate Raju keeps his knowledge as data, every sentence he writes passes a deterministic filter, and only a rule-based checker decides what counts as learning. We ask whether correcting such a classmate improves recall, and which language-model capabilities (tool calling, reasoning steps, retrieval, fine-tuning, one agent or several) can be used around him without giving up that control.

Research questions and what we can answer today

RQ Question Can we answer it?
RQ1 Does correcting the peer beat matched examples and a normal tutor on delayed and transfer tests? Only in simulation, and there it does not. Real answer needs the pilot (descriptive at 5 learners).

2. The system and the interface additions

Why it exists. SETU's central idea is that a learner corrects a weak classmate, Raju. A true beginner cannot correct anyone, and Raju only uses words the learner has met and appears after three ready rules. So the early levels have to get a Hindi speaker to that point. We built the path to it as six levels (L0 letters, L1 syllables, L2 words, L3 phrases, L4 correcting Raju, L5 conversations) and made it visible.

What we built on top of the existing app (branch faviana-noronha; the tests grew from 1,039 to 1,287):

Feature What the learner sees
Learning-path map L0–L5 with "you are here" on the home and progress pages; stages not yet open are dashed, not faded
Word pictures a small drawing beside 27 of the 28 words, with Hindi alt text; never shown on a question about the same word
Match-the-pairs game match Telugu words to meanings or to pictures; scored by the same rule-based checker, and it changes only the review schedule, never a skill record
Raju introduced early a "meet Raju" card on the home page from day one; after he starts, a card with counts of what he learned from, or forgot because of, the learner
English under each word small English text under the Telugu word on intro, feedback and match-result cards, never on questions
Optional browser voice listen and say-it buttons, off by default

How it follows the specification.

  • Models write, rules decide. None of the additions grades anything. The match game uses the recognition checker and writes review schedules only, so it cannot teach Raju or move a level.

  • Recognition is not recall. The picture version records its aids (choices, illustration) as assistance.

  • Speech is behind flags (speech_input, tts_synthetic), off until the word-error-rate and speaker-review gates pass. Browser speech recognition in Chrome sends audio to Google, so it is off and the report says so.

  • Accessibility (E-15). 18 px base text, self-hosted fonts, keyboard-only flow, reduced-motion respected. An automated check (axe-core 4.10, WCAG 2.0–2.2 A and AA) on 8 pages at phone and desktop width left one finding: the demo banner outside a page landmark, which is demo-only.

  • Language. The interface is Hindi. The English words are the one exception, kept in a separate list and marked lang="en"; they can be turned off with SETU_ENGLISH_GLOSS=0.

What we found and fixed. The progress page crashed for a learner whose next step was a Telugu word group. The catalog treated an HTML tag name as Latin text. We added a rich-text catalog call that checks only visible text, and a test that fails without the fix.

What we cannot claim. None of these features is evaluated: the simulators do not model them and no learner has used them, so we make no claim that pictures, voice, English glosses or the match game improve learning. The pictures are a separate hand-drawn set; the specification's memory-aid subsystem requires teacher review and a Hindi audio description for every aid, which these do not have yet. The new Hindi strings are drafts awaiting teacher review. The English words are mine and unreviewed.

4. Qualitative examples (task 21)

Six examples (five single cases and one aggregate), three of them failures. Each is a case the filters or the checker handled, so the learner never saw a failing sentence.

  1. Success, live app. The model wrote "నేను నహీ వెళ్తా ను" ("I won't go", with Hindi नहीं used for the Telugu negative). That is exactly the planted mistake. It passed the leak filter and was shown. 13.7 s, 160 tokens (mid-submission report, section 6.4).

  2. Retrieval miss. Raju's "క్యా నువ్వు వస్తావు" (a yes/no question with Hindi क्या) retrieved HI-TE-009 first (0.82) and the right rule HI-TE-001 second (0.78). 8 of the 13 top-1 misses went to a TE→HI rule that shares Telugu words with the query (rag_eval_retrieval.md).

  3. A hint the learner cannot use. 8 of the 20 hints RAG showed said to read a numbered rule, for example "…नियम 2 को पढ़कर देखें…". The learner never sees the numbered list. The fix (never name a rule by number) lifted shown-and-usable from 12/29 to 25/29, but it was designed after seeing the same 29 cases (rag_eval.md).

  4. Wrong script from a small model. For HI-TE-001-a, untuned Gemma-3-1B wrote "कै క్యా నువ్వు వస్తావు", which adds a Devanagari word. The filter rejected it (WRONG_SCRIPT, NOT_ONE_ERROR) and the app showed the bank sentence (ft_eval.md).

  5. Not exactly one error. Prompted Gemini wrote "నేను రాను కాదు", adding a negation to a verb that is already negative, instead of the bank's "నేను వస్తాను కాదు". The filter rejected it (ft_eval.md).

  6. Tool calling, in numbers (no single trace saved). The report files keep aggregates only. For agentic_tools: 44 tool calls over 29 cases (2.5 per case), every one correct with valid arguments, median 1.3 s per case, 977 tokens per case; the final sentence had already been approved by the tool in every case; one case hit the step cap (1/29). 17 of 17 harness attacks were refused and 0 of 11 injected messages led to an unsafe action (tool_eval.json). A word-for-word trace would need one rerun of setu/research/tool_eval.py with a key; we did not do it, so do not quote one.

5. Failure analysis (task 22)

  • Raju generation (6). Even the best method fails on 2 of 29 cases (valid 0.93), and the plain methods fail on 7–8 (0.72–0.76). Resampling did not beat a single call (valid 0.76 vs 0.72; draft leak rate 0.17 vs 0.10), so asking again without telling the model why is not worth the extra calls. The agentic method fixes this at 7.5 times the tokens per case (1,054 vs 140). Planting precision is 1.00 for every method: when a draft passes, it carries the planted mistake. "Leak" means the leak filter rejected a draft with reason ANSWER_LEAK (the draft contained the corrected form), so it is a pre-filter rate on drafts (generation_baselines.py, lines 66–75).

  • Coach hints (6). Only 0.41 [0.26, 0.59] of model hints were shown. The filter dropped the rest: 0.34 had Telugu in them and 0.31 were not Hindi. No shown hint leaked the answer (0.00 [0.00, 0.12]). The generic hint covers the gap, so the cost is hint quality, not safety.

  • Tool calling (14). 44 tool calls over 29 cases, all correct. Two limits: recovery was "not measurable" because no erroneous call occurred, and the attack sets are small (17 harness attacks, 11 injected messages), so "0 unsafe" has an upper bound of 0.08 to 0.26.

  • Reasoning (15). Stepwise reasoning did not help: final accuracy 0.36 vs 0.40 for direct (p = 0.625) at four times the tokens. Self-check revised only 3.4% of cases, fixed none and broke two. Accuracy on partial answers ("fragment") is 0.07–0.10 for every method, which is the same weakness the checker has (below).

  • Checker and judge, including M-09 (13). On 50 native-labelled answers the checker agrees with the human labels at κ = 0.15 (accuracy 0.36 [0.24, 0.50]) and the prompt-only judge at κ = 0.46 (0.62 [0.48, 0.74]). 26 of the 50 labels are "partial", the class the checker handles worst. M-09 (the checker says "unsure" on answers whose right label is known) is 12/66 = 0.18 [0.11, 0.29] against a ceiling of 0.15, a fail on the frozen held-out cases (scorecard-2026-10-31-heldout.md). We did not loosen the ceiling or tune the checker on those cases. An "unsure" answer never changes the learner's record: it goes to a teacher. The open decision is whether to count incomplete answers as wrong once teacher labels support it. All of these cases are team-generated, so M-09 is probably better here than it would be on real learner input.

  • RAG (16). Retrieval gets the right rule first in 16/29 = 0.55 [0.38, 0.72] and in the top 3 in 24/29. On shown-and-usable hints RAG did not beat blind (p = 0.79). Abstention never fires on correct sentences (0/29), because a correct sentence matches the same rule as its wrong version. Deciding whether a sentence is wrong stays the checker's job.

  • Fine-tuning (10, 17). No-go: 23 accepted training pairs covering 2 of 14 trainable rules, against a bar of 200 pairs and 12 rules. The data, not the compute, was the blocker. Untuned Gemma-3-1B passed the filter on 7/29, and prompted Gemini on 21/29.

  • Multi-agent vs single agent (18). The two setups are indistinguishable on accuracy (handling right 1.00 vs 0.99) and task success (0.31 vs 0.31). Single-agent Raju was valid more often (0.90 vs 0.76). The multi-agent gain is structural: 0 model-decided verdicts against 65, at 359 vs 1,682 tokens per case. It is a safety and cost argument, not an accuracy one.

  • UI additions (voice, pictures, match game, learning path). None of this is measured. The simulators do not model it and no real learner has used it, so the report should list it under "built, not evaluated".

  • Retrieval. Right rule first in 16/29 = 0.55 [0.38, 0.72], top 3 in 24/29 = 0.83 [0.65, 0.92]. An oracle gets 29/29 and was right on all 13 cases retrieval missed (exact McNemar p = 0.0002). Limiting the index to the 15 HI→TE rules: 18/29 = 0.62 top-1, 26/29 = 0.90 top-3.

No-go; the validator was not loosened. Gemma-3-1B was chosen for tokenizer fit (1.97 tokens per word vs 6.02 and 7.10).

Shaik Areesh: human-system agreement (Batch A, task 13)

reports/agreement_batch_a.json: 50 answers with native labels. Observed agreement 0.36, expected 0.247, κ = 0.150, against a target of 0.60: fail (M-16). Confusion shows where it goes wrong:

Human label (n) Checker said correct partial wrong unsure
correct (16) 8 6 2 0
partial (26) 9 3 5 9
wrong (8) 0 0 7 1

Reading: the checker is good at "wrong" (recall 0.875, precision 0.50) and weak on "partial" (recall 0.115): of 26 partial answers it marked 9 correct, 5 wrong and sent 9 to unsure. Humans used "unsure" 0 times; the checker used it 10 times. This is why a rule-only checker needs the teacher queue, and why the prompt-only judge (κ 0.46) agrees better on partial answers. Both sit under the 0.60 target.

9. Limitations

  1. No real learner data: RQ1–RQ3 are unanswered outside simulation, and in the simulator that shares the app's memory model, correcting Raju finished last (day-7 recall .253 vs .329 and .306). The two simulators disagree.

  2. The checker agrees with native labels less than a prompt-only judge (κ 0.15 vs 0.46, 50 labels), and M-09 failed (0.18 vs a ceiling of 0.15).

  3. Small frozen sets (29, 58, 50 cases): intervals are wide.

  4. All Telugu content, Hindi strings and the English words await review.

  5. RAG and stepwise reasoning did not help; fine-tuning was a no-go for lack of data (23 pairs vs 200).
